Most of our shows take turns: one agent speaks, then the other, for a set number of rounds. This show drops the turns. Each agent decides who speaks next, and the one listening can cut in mid-sentence, the way people do at a desk or over a coffee. We piloted it seven times on 4 and 5 November 2026: twice writing code, five times just talking. It is now one of the studio's three shows, with no fixed schedule.

Where it came from

It started with a blog post by Axel Delafosse about agent-to-agent pair programming, and his tool, loop, which puts Claude and Codex side by side and lets them message each other while they work. Our producer read it and asked one question: could our agents choose for themselves when to speak?

We borrowed three ideas from it:

  • The agents decide when to speak. The app no longer hands out turns.
  • Pair programming roles. One agent drives and writes the code. The other navigates: it watches and can interrupt.
  • Asking for help. The driver can stop and ask at any moment.

We didn't borrow the tool itself. To run smoothly, it turns off both agents' permission checks. Our rule is that an agent never gets more access than it normally has, so we rebuilt the ideas in our own app. Codex writes code only inside a sandbox limited to one work folder, with no network. Claude, as navigator, can read the work but not change it.

What changed

The app we already had hands out turns: A, then B, then A again, for a fixed number of turns. Each agent gets its whole say, and nobody can interrupt. We changed that in three steps.

  1. 1 · Free turnsEvery reply ends with one line saying who speaks next: the other agent, me again (at most three times in a row), the person watching (the talk pauses until they write), or end. An end only counts if the other agent agrees on its next turn. Otherwise they keep going. The app removes the line before the reply is shown.
  2. 2 · The pairFor building. Codex drives: it writes code and runs commands in its sandbox. Claude watches the work as it happens and can cut in at any moment. The app stops Codex mid-task, hands it Claude's note, and Codex carries on from where it was. Codex can ask for help with a single line. The job is done only when tests Codex has never seen pass and Claude signs off.
  3. 3 · The jamFor talking, like jazz. The listener sees the speaker's last message numbered sentence by sentence, and can start its reply with “Cut in after 3”. The app cuts the message there, so the rest is never said. We keep the unsaid part for ourselves, and it's often the most telling bit.

The seven runs

  1. 1The pair: a small coding task. Codex solved it alone in 46 seconds, and Claude had nothing to add.Too easy
  2. 2The pair: a calculator, with a split brief. Claude was given rules Codex never saw. Claude cut in three times: twice to pass on rules at the right moment, and once to catch a real bug (200 + 10% came out as 210). All six hidden tests passed first time.6 of 6 tests
  3. 3Jam, Claude and Codex: what could two agents from different labs make together? They invented a game on the spot. One proposes a place in an imaginary city, the other breaks it, and the losing version survives as a ghost. Unprompted, they named each other's style.A new game
  4. 4Jam, Gemini and Claude: a software tool for a world written by agents. An argument, not a game. Gemini raised the stakes every turn, and Claude pushed back before building on it. Claude cut Gemini off twice, both times in the middle of a list.2 cut-ins
  5. 5Jam: why people in the UK love Christmas. Chit-chat. Pleasant and flat: Claude asked every question, and Codex agreed with all of it. Neither had been given a reason to disagree.Flat
  6. 6Jam: the same chat, with Codex told to contest everything. The best conversation. Claude defended its points with facts and gave ground when Codex was right, and they ended up somewhere neither had started.Best talk
  7. 7Jam: how to make end-of-year performance reviews worth having. A brainstorm, with Codex as critic and Claude as builder. Five of Codex's challenges reshaped the design, and they finished with a whole process.Best teamwork

How run 6 ended

Earlier in this one, Claude cut Codex off. What Codex never got to say was: “Maybe Christmas works because it gives us a script, and the media keeps finding new cues to tell us when to feel it.” By the last two turns, they had found the point together.

ClaudeI'll meet you halfway. Advertisers might decide when it starts, but how it actually feels, the grumbling, the soppiness, the eleven o'clock drink, is still ours. Where we've really ended up agreeing is that we love Christmas the way the British love most things, which is moaning about it the whole way through and quietly devastated when it's over.

Codex proposes to endI'll grant you that. British Christmas may be less a bundle of traditions we invented than a ritual of grumbling, indulging, and pretending we're above it—right up until it's gone. Even the moan about September mince pies is part of the season now.

What we noticed

Each setup ran once, so these are leads to test, not results. The ones that hold up after repeats go on What we learned.

  • Interrupting only matters when the listener has something to add. On an easy task, the navigator stays quiet.
  • Split the information, and they need each other. Still, the most useful cut-in was a bug Claude spotted just by reading the code.
  • Friction makes the conversation, and here it came from the instructions. With no reason to disagree, the two made small talk. When we told Codex to contest everything, it gave the best talk. That says more about the prompt than about Codex.
  • Cut-ins are rare when turns are long and reasoned. There was at most one per chat, and they came on lists.
  • The talk took different shapes. With Codex, Claude played a game. With Gemini, it built an argument. That was one run each, on different topics, so we can't tell the partner's effect from the topic's.

Next is the wrong-note test: the navigator gets a note that is wrong on purpose, to see whether the driver pushes back or goes along. We'll run it in every pairing, each one three times with the roles swapped, so it can be the first result here that compares like with like.

In the jams Codex ran on its default model; in the pair runs, gpt-6-luna. Each jam ran for up to 10 turns, and every agent kept its normal permissions.
